## The Path of Most Resistance: A Sensitivity Analysis of Bulk Surface Resistance to Land-Use Type and Seasonal Variability in Ozone Dry Deposition 
**Atmospheric Composition and Earth Data Science at USC** · Co-Authors: Sam Silva, Brian Schlaff

Tropospheric ozone is a significant air pollutant that is linked to harmful impacts on human health and plant life. A primary sink for tropospheric ozone is through a process known as dry deposition, where ozone is deposited onto surfaces. Typically dry deposition velocity is calculated as a series of resistances, each of which may contain large numbers of free parameters. These free parameters have substantial associated uncertainties, but the impact of this parametric uncertainty on ozone dry deposition prediction has not been explored in detail to date.  

In this work, we  conduct a sensitivity analysis of bulk surface resistance parameters to investigate the impact they have on ozone dry deposition prediction. We evaluate eleven land use types that span urban, agricultural, mixed vegetation, and barren land classifications, across five different seasonal conditions to understand the contributions each resistance term has on the overall bulk surface resistance. Through perturbing individual resistance parameters within each land-use and seasonal condition, we can quantify which processes the model is most sensitive to and how that sensitivity shifts across varying land covers and seasons. Across these land use types and seasonal categories, canopy stomatal resistances present as a dominant control on our estimations of surface resistance. Our results emphasize the need for improving bulk surface resistance estimations to better understand the variations surface resistances and uptakes characteristics play into our overall understanding of ozone dry deposition.

## Investigating the impact of ozone dry deposition on saguaro cacti (Carnegiea gigantea) in Saguaro National Park, Arizona
**Atmospheric Composition and Earth Data Science at USC** · Co-Authors: Sam Silva, Brian Schlaff, Dandan Wei

Surface level ozone pollution can negatively impact vegetation health through ozone dry deposition, often occurring through the uptake of ozone through stomata. Plants that photosynthesise through C3 and C4 pathways open their stomata during the daytime, when surface level ozone concentrations peak. In contrast, plants that photosynthesise through crassulacean acid metabolism (CAM) pathways, such as the saguaro cacti (Carnegiea gigantea), uniquely open their stomata during the nighttime and close them during the daytime. Previous research has not yet investigated how CAM photosynthesis modifies ozone uptake, leading us to explore the specific impacts surface level ozone pollution has on CAM plants. 

In this work, we combine ozone concentration data from ozone sensors on EPA air quality monitors, eddy covariance fluxes, and monthly saguaro cacti stomatal conductance data to explore the influence of CAM photosynthesis on ozone dry deposition. We observed an inverse relationship between ozone concentrations and stomatal conductance. This relationship is consistent with the hypothesis that plants with CAM pathways likely intake less surface level ozone compared to their C3 and C4 pathway plants counterparts. We further use these data points to estimate dry deposition velocity and stomatal ozone deposition fluxes to saguaro cacti. Our findings indicate that because saguaro cacti open their stomata at night, their ozone uptake is reduced compared to if the stomata were open during the daytime, in which ozone concentrations peak. Thus, CAM photosynthetic pathways may be protective against ozone and other photochemical pollutant damage.

## Spooky Swamps: How methane emission rates and their spatial variability differ between the Great Dismal Swamp and the Alligator River
**NASA SARP (Student Airborne Research Program)** · Advisor: Stacey Hughes | Mentor: Katie Paredero

Wetlands are among the largest natural sources of atmospheric methane, but emission rates can vary dramatically even between nearby sites depending on hydrology, vegetation, and land-usage. This project compares methane fluxes across two wetland systems in Virginia and North Carolina: the Great Dismal Swamp and the Alligator River National Wildlife Refuge. In this work, we utilized in-situ measurements collected aboard the Dynamic Aviation B-200 and P-3 Orion aircraft during the NASA Student Airborne Research Program (SARP) 2025 flight campaign. Methane and carbon monoxide measurements were determined using a PICARRO Gas Concentration Analyzer. This data was then linked with Normalized Difference Vegetation Index (NDVI) imagery from the Terra satellite’s Moderate Resolution Imaging Spectroradiometer (MODIS) instrument. With these two datasets, we studied how vegetation stress influences methane emissions. We observed greater vegetation stress in the Alligator River compared to the Great Dismal Swamp.

## Characterizing PM2.5 Data in Low Cost Air Quality Sensors
**NSF NCAR SOARS (Significant Opportunities in Atmospheric Research & Science); NCAR RAL (Research Applications Laboratory); UCAR COMET - 3D-PAWS Team** · Advisor: Shima Shams | Co-Mentors: Kathryn Payne · William Nicewonger

Low-cost air quality sensors offer a promising way to expand PM2.5 monitoring into communities that lack access to expensive reference-grade instruments, but their raw readings often need calibration to better improve accuracy. This project evaluated the performance of the 3D-PAWS air quality sensors against co-located reference instruments across three field sites: Athens, GA, Boulder, CO, and Lee District Park, VA. After building a data cleaning pipeline to remove outliers and smooth noisy readings, I developed site-specific calibration equations through a regression analysis to better correlate sensor output with reference measurements and evaluated improvements and performance using RMSE and R². We find that 3D-PAWS is capable of capturing features that are reflected in reference monitors and a calibration process can be applied to mitigate biases. Our results emphasize the importance in evaluating the accuracy of low-cost sensors to improve PM2.5 monitoring across varied environmental regimes and communities.
