## Spooky Swamps: How methane emission rates and their spatial variability differ between the Great Dismal Swamp and the Alligator River
**NASA SARP (Student Airborne Research Program)** · Advisor: Stacey Hughes | Mentor: Katie Paredero

Wetlands are among the largest natural sources of atmospheric methane, but emission rates can vary dramatically even between nearby sites depending on hydrology, vegetation, and land-usage. This project compares methane fluxes across two wetland systems in Virginia and North Carolina: the Great Dismal Swamp and the Alligator River National Wildlife Refuge. In this work, we utilized in-situ measurements collected aboard the Dynamic Aviation B-200 and P-3 Orion aircraft during the NASA Student Airborne Research Program (SARP) 2025 flight campaign. Methane and carbon monoxide measurements were determined using a PICARRO Gas Concentration Analyzer. This data was then linked with Normalized Difference Vegetation Index (NDVI) imagery from the Terra satellite’s Moderate Resolution Imaging Spectroradiometer (MODIS) instrument. With these two datasets, we studied how vegetation stress influences methane emissions. We observed greater vegetation stress in the Alligator River compared to the Great Dismal Swamp.

## Characterizing PM2.5 Data in Low Cost Air Quality Sensors
**NSF NCAR SOARS (Significant Opportunities in Atmospheric Research & Science); NCAR RAL (Research Applications Laboratory); UCAR COMET - 3D-PAWS Team** · Advisor: Shima Shams | Co-Mentors: Kathryn Payne · William Nicewonger

Low-cost air quality sensors offer a promising way to expand PM2.5 monitoring into communities that lack access to expensive reference-grade instruments, but their raw readings often need calibration to better improve accuracy. This project evaluated the performance of the 3D-PAWS air quality sensors against co-located reference instruments across three field sites: Athens, GA, Boulder, CO, and Lee District Park, VA. After building a data cleaning pipeline to remove outliers and smooth noisy readings, I developed site-specific calibration equations through a regression analysis to better correlate sensor output with reference measurements and evaluated improvements and performance using RMSE and R². We find that 3D-PAWS is capable of capturing features that are reflected in reference monitors and a calibration process can be applied to mitigate biases. Our results emphasize the importance in evaluating the accuracy of low-cost sensors to improve PM2.5 monitoring across varied environmental regimes and communities.
